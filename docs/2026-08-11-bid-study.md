## 0 · The one-sentence version

Bids are the busiest write path in the account — **23,269 changes in 60 days across 614 targets** —

```ts
// automation-action-handlers.ts:136
function applyBidPercent(currentCents: number, percent: number): number {
  const next = Math.round(currentCents * (1 + percent / 100))
  return Math.max(BID_FLOOR_CENTS, next)          // ← a real floor, unlike the budget path
}
```

**This is the same "percent of the current value" shape that ratcheted budgets to €1 in study 6 —
but with a floor constant and, as measured below, a very different cadence.**

**Five rules are enabled AND on AUTO** — the largest live automation surface in the account.

Three observations:

🔴 **"Weekend budget boost" is on AUTO and its action is `bid_to_target_acos`.** It is a *bid* rule
wearing a budget rule's name, writing autonomously, 4,829 times. Whatever it does, nobody reading
the rule list would predict it.

🔴 **Six `bid_to_target_acos` rules, five of them live, three on AUTO.** They all converge the same
number by the same mechanism, unscoped, across the whole account. Nothing states which wins when
two disagree on the same target in the same tick. *(The earlier research note stands: Amazon now
gives dynamic bidding and auto-targeting free, which makes six overlapping target-ACoS rules the
least differentiated thing here.)*

⚠️ **`bid_down` stores a positive percent and negates it in the handler** (`-Math.abs(percent)`).
So the rule list renders *"+25%"* for a rule that cuts bids 25%. The behaviour is right; the display
is backwards.

## 4 · Is there a bid ratchet? — yes, and it is a different animal

179 chains of **three or more consecutive cuts** on one target. The longest:

| target | cuts | from | to | over |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cmr28mgl5… | 19 | €0.38 | **€0.02** | **433 h** |
| cmr28m8s5… | 19 | €0.38 | €0.02 | 433 h |
| …ten more, identical | 19 | €0.38 | €0.02 | 433 h |

**433 hours is 18 days — roughly one cut per day, ≈ −15% a step.** `0.38 × 0.85¹⁹ ≈ 0.017`, which
floors at 2¢. Twelve targets moved in lockstep, so they are being driven as a group.

**This is not the budget failure.** Budgets fell €4.42 → €1.00 in **2¾ hours** through compounding
15-minute ticks with no floor above Amazon's minimum. Bids fall over **18 days**, one step a day,
into a floor the code enforces deliberately. That is a controlled ease-down, and for a keyword that
genuinely underperforms it is correct behaviour.

**What is missing is not a brake — it is a statement.** Nothing anywhere says "this keyword has been
cut nineteen times and now sits at the floor; it is effectively off." Twelve of them at once is a
decision about a product line, made a percent at a time, and visible only in a log nobody reads.

**28% of live keywords sit at 2¢.** That is not damage — it is the standing "never pause, suppress
with ~€0.02 bids" policy executing correctly, driven by the rank engine's `pause` target, whose
`floorBidCents` defaults to *"the engine's legacy 2¢ constant"* (study 5). Study 5 also measured all
33 live schedules holding `pause` at 00:45, so a night-time reading would show even more.

⚠️ **The €2.05 maximum matches a known open item** — 70 Sponsored Brands keywords drifted to €2.05
on Amazon and were never healed. 35 keywords sit above €1.00; that drift is plausibly most of them.

## 6 · 🔴 The bounds that should govern all this are unset

Every bid write passes `ads-write-gate.ts`, which enforces per-campaign bounds:

| bound | campaigns with it set |
|---|---|
| **`minBidCents`** | **0 of 220** |
| `maxBidCents` | 82 of 220 — *exactly the 82 with the write gate open* |
| `pinBids` (hands off) | **0** |

`maxBidCents` exists only where writes are permitted, which suggests it was set by the same process
that opened the gate rather than as a deliberate per-campaign ceiling. **`minBidCents` is set
nowhere at all** — so the only floor in the system is the code constant, and no campaign can declare
"never take my bids below X".

**Study 6's conclusion generalises:** in this account, the guardrails are code constants and
execution caps, not the per-entity bounds the architecture was built to use. The columns exist, are
enforced, and are empty — and there is no UI to fill them.

## 7 · How the industry does this

### 7.1 The platforms

| platform | bid approach | price point |
|---|---|---|
| **Quartile** | **hourly bid adjustments** on patented algorithms | $895+/mo — the most expensive here, explicitly enterprise |
| **Pacvue** | deep rule automation + programmatic across many retailers | $500+/mo |
| **Perpetua** | **goal-based** — declare a target ACoS or growth goal and the algorithm works backwards; built for operators who want to be hands-off | mid |
| **Teikametrics** (Flywheel 2.0) | ML bidding since 2015, Amazon + Walmart | $149+/mo |
| **Scale Insights · Ad Badger** | rules you write; Ad Badger adjusts hourly | low |

### 7.2 The one line worth the whole search

> *"The biggest differentiator in 2026 is not bid algorithms. It is whether your PPC software reacts
> to inventory, pricing, and margin changes automatically."*

By that standard **this system is ahead of most of the field**, not behind it. It already has
`retail_guard`, `pause_ads_for_product`, FBA-stock rules, `trueProfitCents` on the campaign and a
profit-native optimiser. What it lacks is not intelligence — it is **legibility**.

### 7.3 What the field has that we do not

1. **One bidder, not six.** Every platform above runs a single bid engine with one goal per
   campaign. We have six overlapping `bid_to_target_acos` rules, three on AUTO, plus a rank engine
   that outwrites them all — and no precedence.
2. **Hourly as a deliberate choice.** Quartile charges a premium for hourly bidding. Ours is
   effectively continuous by accident, because several rules tick every 15 minutes with only an
   executions-per-day cap between them and the account.
3. **A bid history per keyword.** Every tool shows the bid curve with the reasons on it. We have
   23,269 rows of exactly that and no chart.
4. **Bounds as a first-class control.** Per-campaign or per-keyword min/max is standard. Ours exists
   in the schema, is enforced at the gate, and is set on zero campaigns.
5. **A stated model.** Perpetua tells you it works backwards from your goal. Nothing here tells an
   operator what `bid_to_target_acos` will do to a keyword tomorrow.

### 7.4 The UI shape they converge on

- **A bid curve per keyword** — the last N changes with the actor and reason on each point.
- **Bid vs suggested bid vs the winning range**, so a number has context.
- **Min/max as visible per-row fields**, editable in bulk.
- **One "who is bidding on this" line** naming the single engine in charge.
- **A staged-changes tray**: what the engine will do next tick, approvable before it happens. *(We
  have one of these — in `ads-console/rank`, the console being retired.)*

## 8 · What could be implemented, cheapest first

### Tier 0 — say what is happening *(hours)*
- **Show the bid history.** 23,269 rows exist; nothing renders one. A per-keyword curve with actor
  and reason is the single highest-value addition to this tab.
- **Surface the 865 failed writes.**
- **Fix the `bid_down` display** — a rule that cuts 25% should not read "+25%".
- **Rename "Weekend budget boost"**, which is on AUTO and changes bids.
- **Flag targets at the floor**: 557 keywords at 2¢, and 179 of them arrived there through a chain
  of cuts nobody was told about.

### Tier 1 — establish who is in charge *(days)*
- **Name a single bidder per campaign.** Six target-ACoS rules and a rank engine writing the same
  targets is not a strategy; whichever ran last wins.
- **Fill in `minBidCents`.** Zero campaigns declare a floor, so the only floor is a constant.
- **Give `ad-rank-defend` a surface** — it makes the bid writes *and* the placement writes. Two tabs
  describe it and neither shows it.

### Tier 2 — the cadence question
Six rules ticking every 15 minutes, bounded only by executions-per-day, is hourly bidding without
having chosen hourly bidding. Either make it deliberate — with the hourly data from study 4 behind
it — or slow the cutters to daily, as their evidence window already implies.

### Tier 3 — one goal per campaign
The Perpetua model: declare the goal, let one engine own the execution, keep the rules for
exceptions. The substrate is already here — `RankTarget` is exactly a goal object, and
`trueProfitCents` makes the profit-native path genuinely differentiated.

## 9 · How this tab is *supposed* to be

> **One question: what is each keyword bidding, why is it that number, and who decided?**

- **A bid curve per keyword**, annotated with actor and reason.
- **One named bidder per campaign**, with rules as declared exceptions.
- **Floors and ceilings visible and editable** on the row they govern.
- **Chains announced**: "cut 19 times in 18 days, now at floor" is a fact that should find the
  operator, not wait to be queried.
- **Failures visible** — 865 of them.
- **The suppression floor labelled as policy**, so 28% of keywords at 2¢ reads as intent rather than
  collapse.
